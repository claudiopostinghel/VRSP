The Last Signal

Captain Mara Chen had not slept in thirty-one hours. The dim blue glow of the navigation console was the only light on the bridge of the Artemis, a deep-space research vessel drifting three hundred light-years from Earth. Around her, the ship hummed with the low vibration of its fusion core, a sound she had learned to find comforting during her five years in space. But tonight, nothing was comforting.

The signal had arrived at 02:47 station time. A single burst of data, compressed and encrypted in a format that no human system recognized. It had come from a point in empty space where no star, no planet, and no station existed. The ship's computer had flagged it automatically, and Mara had been staring at it ever since.

"Run the analysis again," she told the computer.

"Analysis complete. The signal contains a repeating mathematical sequence based on prime numbers. The encoding does not match any known human or natural source. Origin point confirmed: coordinates 47.3 by negative 12.8 by 201.6. No catalogued objects exist at that location."

Mara leaned back in her chair and rubbed her eyes. She had spent most of her career studying cosmic background radiation and occasional bursts from distant pulsars. She had never expected to find anything like this. The prime number sequence was too deliberate, too structured to be random noise. Someone, or something, had sent this message on purpose.

She opened a channel to the crew quarters. "Dr. Okafor, please come to the bridge."

Three minutes later, Aiden Okafor appeared, still pulling on his jacket. He was the ship's linguist and communications specialist, brought along for the unlikely possibility that the Artemis might detect something worth translating. Until now, his job had been mostly theoretical.

"What is it?" he asked, yawning.

Mara pointed at the screen. Aiden sat down and began scrolling through the data. His yawn disappeared. His eyes widened. He looked at her, then back at the screen, then at her again.

"This is structured," he said quietly.

"I know."

"This is not natural."

"I know."

He pulled up a secondary display and began running his own tools against the signal. For twenty minutes, neither of them spoke. The ship drifted silently through the void while two humans tried to make sense of something that should not exist.

"There are layers," Aiden finally said. "The prime sequence is just the outer shell. Beneath it, there is a grid pattern. It could be an image. Or a map. I need more time."

Mara nodded. "Take whatever time you need. I am going to adjust our course toward the origin point."

Aiden looked up sharply. "You want to fly toward it?"

"We are the only ship within fifty light-years of that location. If we do not investigate, nobody will. Not for decades."

He hesitated, then nodded slowly. "Alright. But we should inform Earth."

"I already sent a report. At this distance, it will take over three hundred years to arrive. We are on our own."

The course adjustment took the Artemis four days to complete. During that time, Aiden worked continuously on the signal. He decoded the grid pattern and confirmed that it was indeed an image. It showed a simple diagram: a circle with eight lines radiating outward, like a star or a sun. Below it, a series of dots arranged in groups. Two dots, then three, then five, then seven. Primes again.

On the fifth day, they reached the coordinates. There was nothing visible. No object, no debris, no energy signature. Just empty space.

"Could the signal have originated from somewhere else and bounced off something here?" Mara asked.

"No. The direction and timing are precise. It came from this exact point."

They waited. Mara ordered a full sensor sweep every hour. For two days, they found nothing. On the third day, the sensors detected a faint gravitational anomaly, something with mass but no size. It was not a black hole. It was not dark matter. It was something the instruments could detect but not explain.

"It is like a door," Aiden said, staring at his readings. "A point in space that has weight but no volume. That should be impossible."

"And yet here it is."

On the fourth day, a second signal arrived. It came from the anomaly, and this time it was different. The data was denser, more complex. Aiden spent twelve hours decoding it. When he was finished, he sat in silence for a long time.

"Well?" Mara asked.

"It is a language primer," he said. "Whoever sent this is teaching us how to talk to them. Basic concepts first. Numbers, directions, elements. Then more complex ideas. Time. Distance. Questions."

"Questions?"

"The last section of the signal is a question. As far as I can translate, it asks something like: Are you ready?"

Mara felt a chill run through her. She looked out the bridge window at the empty stars. Somewhere in that darkness, something was waiting for an answer.

"Ready for what?" she whispered.

Aiden shook his head. "I do not know. But I think they have been asking for a very long time. This anomaly, this door, it might have been here for thousands of years. Maybe longer. Waiting for someone to come close enough to hear."

Mara thought about Earth, three hundred light-years away. She thought about every radio signal humanity had ever sent into the cosmos, all those hopeful messages flung into the dark. And now, finally, something had answered.

She sat down at the communications console and opened a new channel, directed at the anomaly. Her fingers hovered over the keys.

"What do we say?" Aiden asked.

Mara took a deep breath. The weight of the moment pressed down on her. Every word she typed would be the first sentence in a conversation between humanity and the unknown. She could not afford to get it wrong. But she also could not afford to stay silent.

She typed a single line and transmitted it before she could change her mind.

We are here.

For thirty seconds, nothing happened. Then the anomaly pulsed, a brief flash of light that their instruments barely registered. And then, slowly, the point in space began to open.

Neither of them moved. Neither of them breathed. The stars around the anomaly seemed to bend and stretch, as if space itself was unfolding. Through the opening, they could see something impossible: a sky full of colors that had no names, and shapes that did not belong to any geometry they had ever studied.

The ship's computer chimed softly. "Incoming transmission."

The message was short. Aiden translated it in seconds.

We have been waiting. Come.

Mara looked at Aiden. He looked at her. In that single glance, they shared everything: the fear, the wonder, the understanding that their lives had just changed forever.

She reached for the navigation controls and set a new course, straight ahead, into the unknown.

Behind them, the stars of the Milky Way shone on in silence. The Artemis moved forward, a tiny spark of light crossing the threshold between everything humanity had ever known and everything it had yet to discover.

The door closed behind them. And the universe, for the first time in a very long while, was no longer empty.
